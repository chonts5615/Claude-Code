## Design Principles for Question Stems

The following principles govern all question stem construction. Deviations from these principles degrade criterion-related validity and introduce construct-irrelevant variance into the interview scoring process.

### Principle 1: Date-Anchor to Force Specificity

Every stem must contain a temporal anchor that compels the candidate to retrieve a discrete, verifiable episode rather than fabricate an idealized composite narrative. Standard anchors include "in the last 12-18 months," "within the past year," and "during your time at [most recent role]." For JL1 candidates with shorter tenure, the anchor may extend to 24 months. For University Recruiting, anchors reference "during your academic program" or "within the last two years."

### Principle 2: Use Action-Forcing Language

Stems must open with phrases that demand narrative reconstruction of actual behavior: "Walk me through," "Tell me about a specific time," "Take me through the sequence of events when," or "Give me a concrete example of a situation where." These constructions force the candidate into the Situation-Task-Action-Result (STAR) framework without explicitly naming it, thereby reducing rehearsed, formulaic responses.

### Principle 3: Avoid Leading Language That Telegraphs the Desired Answer

A stem must never embed the competency label or the socially desirable response within the question itself. Asking "Tell me about a time you demonstrated excellent leadership" reveals the scoring dimension and invites self-serving attribution. The stem should instead describe the situational conditions and let the candidate's behavioral response reveal the competency organically.

### Principle 4: Avoid Hypothetical Framing Disguised as Behavioral

Stems beginning with "What would you do if" or "How would you handle" are situational interview questions, not behavioral event questions. While situational questions have utility, they measure behavioral intention rather than demonstrated behavioral history. This reference file is exclusively concerned with past-behavior stems. Any hypothetical language must be eliminated during guide construction.

### Principle 5: Use Inclusive, Culturally Neutral Language

Given Cargill's 130,000-employee global footprint spanning diverse regulatory, linguistic, and cultural contexts, all stems must avoid idioms, colloquialisms, or culturally specific references that disadvantage non-native English speakers or candidates from non-Western organizational norms. Stems should reference universal workplace constructs (e.g., "a project with competing priorities" rather than "a time you had to burn the midnight oil"). All stems must be evaluated for potential adverse impact against protected demographic groups prior to deployment.

### Principle 6: Embed Probing Architecture

Each primary stem should be constructed to permit natural follow-up probes that deepen the behavioral data without leading the candidate. Effective stems end with embedded secondary probes (e.g., "What was your specific role, and what was the outcome?") that signal to the interviewer where to drill further while maintaining standardization across candidates.

### Principle 7: Calibrate Complexity to Job Level

JL1 stems should reference individual contributor scope (personal decisions, task-level challenges, peer collaboration). JL2 stems should reference team-level accountability and people management. JL3 and JL4 stems should reference enterprise-level impact, cross-functional orchestration, executive stakeholder management, and multi-year strategic horizons. The behavioral signal expected at each level must scale accordingly.

## Question Stem Templates by Competency Category

### 1. Strategic Thinking and Vision

These stems elicit evidence of the candidate's capacity to translate enterprise strategy into operational action, scan the competitive environment for emerging trends, and articulate a compelling vision that aligns teams toward long-range objectives. At JL3 and JL4, stems should surface evidence of market sensing, portfolio-level trade-offs, and the ability to cascade strategic priorities across business units.

**Stem 1.1**
"Walk me through a specific situation in the last 18 months where you had to adjust your team's strategic priorities based on new market information. What data did you rely on, and how did you communicate the shift?"
(Tests: environmental scanning, data-driven decision-making, change communication)

**Stem 1.2**
"Tell me about a time in the past year when you identified a trend or emerging risk in your industry or function before it was widely recognized. What did you do with that insight, and what resulted from your actions?"
(Tests: anticipatory thinking, initiative, translating insight to action)

**Stem 1.3**
"Give me a concrete example of when you had to make a strategic trade-off between two competing priorities, both of which had significant business implications. How did you evaluate the options, and what was the outcome?"
(Tests: prioritization under constraint, analytical reasoning, strategic judgment)

**Stem 1.4**
"Take me through a time when you were responsible for translating a broad organizational goal into specific, measurable objectives for your team or function. What steps did you take, and how did you track progress?"
(Tests: strategy-to-execution translation, goal setting, performance management)

**Stem 1.5**
"Tell me about a specific instance in the last 12 months where you challenged an existing strategic assumption within your organization. What prompted your challenge, and how was it received?"
(Tests: intellectual courage, critical thinking, constructive dissent)

**Stem 1.6**
"Walk me through a situation where you had to develop a plan that spanned more than one fiscal year. What factors did you consider, and how did you maintain alignment as conditions changed over time?"
(Tests: long-range planning, adaptability, stakeholder alignment over extended timelines)

**Stem 1.7**
"Give me an example of when you used data from multiple sources, including sources outside your immediate function, to inform a strategic recommendation. What were the sources, and how did you reconcile conflicting data points?"
(Tests: cross-functional data synthesis, analytical rigor, evidence-based reasoning)

**Stem 1.8**
"Tell me about a time when your initial strategic direction proved to be wrong. How did you recognize the error, and what did you do to correct course?"
(Tests: self-awareness, intellectual humility, adaptive strategy execution)

**Stem 1.9**
"Describe a specific situation where you had to align stakeholders with fundamentally different views on the direction of a project or initiative. What approach did you take to build consensus, and what was the final outcome?"
(Tests: stakeholder alignment, influence without authority, consensus-building)

**Stem 1.10**
"Walk me through a time when you had to evaluate whether to enter a new market, launch a new product, or expand into a new geographic region. What analytical frameworks or data did you use to make the recommendation?"
(Tests: market analysis, risk assessment, strategic recommendation formulation)

### 2. Leading People and Teams

These stems target evidence of the candidate's ability to motivate, develop, and hold team members accountable. At JL2, the focus is on direct team management. At JL3 and JL4, the focus shifts to leading leaders, building organizational capability, and shaping the performance culture across multiple teams or business units.

**Stem 2.1**
"Tell me about a specific time in the last year when you had to give difficult performance feedback to a direct report. Walk me through how you prepared, what you said, and how the individual responded."
(Tests: feedback delivery, interpersonal courage, developmental orientation)

**Stem 2.2**
"Give me an example of when you took over a team that was underperforming. What did you assess first, what actions did you take, and how did performance change over the following months?"
(Tests: diagnostic leadership, turnaround execution, performance management)

**Stem 2.3**
"Walk me through a situation where two members of your team were in significant conflict with each other. What did you observe, how did you intervene, and what was the resolution?"
(Tests: conflict management, mediation, team cohesion maintenance)

**Stem 2.4**
"Tell me about a time when you deliberately structured a team differently, whether by changing roles, adding members, or reorganizing reporting lines, to improve performance. What was your rationale, and what happened?"
(Tests: team composition strategy, organizational design, structural problem-solving)

**Stem 2.5**
"Describe a specific instance where you had to motivate a team through a period of sustained ambiguity or uncertainty, such as an organizational restructuring or a prolonged project without clear direction. What did you do?"
(Tests: motivational leadership under uncertainty, psychological safety creation, resilience)

**Stem 2.6**
"Give me an example of a time when you identified a high-potential individual on your team and took deliberate steps to accelerate their development. What did you do, and where is that person now?"
(Tests: talent identification, developmental investment, succession planning orientation)

**Stem 2.7**
"Tell me about a situation where you had to hold a well-liked or high-performing team member accountable for a behavior or outcome that was not meeting expectations. How did you handle it?"
(Tests: accountability enforcement, fairness, performance standards consistency)

**Stem 2.8**
"Walk me through a time when you led a team that was distributed across multiple locations, time zones, or cultures. What specific practices did you put in place to maintain cohesion and performance?"
(Tests: virtual/distributed team leadership, cross-cultural coordination, communication protocols)

**Stem 2.9**
"Tell me about a time when you had to make a difficult decision about removing someone from a team or from the organization. What factors did you weigh, and how did you execute the decision?"
(Tests: decisive action on people decisions, due process adherence, emotional regulation)

**Stem 2.10**
"Give me an example of when you delegated a critical task or decision to someone who had not previously handled that level of responsibility. How did you prepare them, and what was the result?"
(Tests: delegation calibration, trust-building, developmental stretch assignment design)

### 3. Driving Results and Execution

These stems elicit evidence of the candidate's orientation toward accountability, operational discipline, and the ability to deliver measurable outcomes under constraint. At higher job levels, the focus shifts from personal task completion to orchestrating complex, cross-functional execution.

**Stem 3.1**
"Walk me through a project or initiative in the last 12 months where you were personally accountable for delivering a result on a tight deadline. What obstacles did you encounter, and how did you overcome them?"
(Tests: personal accountability, obstacle navigation, deadline management)

**Stem 3.2**
"Tell me about a time when you had to deliver a critical outcome with fewer resources than you believed were necessary. What trade-offs did you make, and how did the result compare to the original objective?"
(Tests: resource optimization, pragmatic problem-solving, trade-off management)

**Stem 3.3**
"Give me a concrete example of a time when a project you were leading fell behind schedule or off track. How did you identify the problem, and what specific steps did you take to recover?"
(Tests: project recovery, root cause analysis, corrective action execution)

**Stem 3.4**
"Describe a situation where you had to manage multiple competing deliverables simultaneously. How did you prioritize, and what was the outcome for each?"
(Tests: workload management, prioritization discipline, multi-stream coordination)

**Stem 3.5**
"Tell me about a time when you set an ambitious performance target for yourself or your team that others considered unrealistic. What was the target, what happened, and what did you learn?"
(Tests: goal ambition calibration, stretch goal pursuit, reflective learning from outcomes)

**Stem 3.6**
"Walk me through a situation where you implemented a new process, system, or standard operating procedure to improve operational efficiency. What problem were you solving, and how did you measure the improvement?"
(Tests: process improvement orientation, measurement discipline, operational excellence)

**Stem 3.7**
"Give me an example of when you had to make a rapid decision with incomplete information. What was at stake, what did you decide, and how did it turn out?"
(Tests: decision-making under uncertainty, risk tolerance calibration, judgment speed)

**Stem 3.8**
"Tell me about a time when you identified a significant quality or compliance issue in a deliverable before it reached the customer or end user. What did you catch, and what action did you take?"
(Tests: quality vigilance, proactive risk mitigation, attention to detail at scale)

**Stem 3.9**
"Describe a situation where you had to enforce a standard or hold the line on a requirement that was unpopular with your peers or stakeholders. What was the requirement, and how did you handle the pushback?"
(Tests: standards enforcement, professional courage, stakeholder resistance management)

**Stem 3.10**
"Walk me through a time when a result you delivered did not meet expectations. What happened, what was your role in the shortfall, and what did you do afterward?"
(Tests: ownership of failure, self-awareness, corrective learning orientation)

### 4. Managing Change and Innovation

These stems assess the candidate's ability to lead through ambiguity, champion novel approaches, and manage the human dynamics of organizational transformation. At JL3 and JL4, stems should surface evidence of enterprise-scale transformation leadership and the capacity to sustain momentum through prolonged implementation timelines.

**Stem 4.1**
"Tell me about a specific time in the last 18 months when you led your team through a significant organizational change, such as a restructuring, technology migration, or process overhaul. What was your approach to managing resistance?"
(Tests: change leadership, resistance management, stakeholder engagement during transition)

**Stem 4.2**
"Give me an example of when you championed a new idea, process, or technology that was initially met with skepticism. How did you build support, and what happened?"
(Tests: innovation advocacy, influence, persistence in the face of organizational inertia)

**Stem 4.3**
"Walk me through a situation where you had to operate effectively for an extended period without clear direction or defined processes. What did you do to create clarity for yourself or your team?"
(Tests: ambiguity tolerance, self-directed structuring, psychological resilience)

**Stem 4.4**
"Tell me about a time when you intentionally experimented with a new approach, knowing there was a real possibility of failure. What was the experiment, what safeguards did you put in place, and what did you learn?"
(Tests: calculated risk-taking, experimental mindset, learning from controlled failure)

**Stem 4.5**
"Describe a specific instance where you had to help your team or organization abandon a long-standing practice that was no longer effective. How did you make the case for change, and how did people respond?"
(Tests: legacy practice disruption, evidence-based advocacy, emotional intelligence during loss)

**Stem 4.6**
"Give me an example of when you integrated a new technology or tool, including AI-based tools, into your team's workflow. What problem did it solve, how did you manage the adoption, and what unexpected challenges arose?"
(Tests: technology adoption leadership, practical implementation capability, human-machine teaming)

**Stem 4.7**
"Tell me about a time when an organizational change you were part of did not go as planned. What went wrong, what was your role, and what would you do differently?"
(Tests: change management failure analysis, retrospective learning, accountability in transformation)

**Stem 4.8**
"Walk me through a situation where you had to sustain team energy and commitment through a change initiative that took significantly longer than originally planned. What strategies did you use?"
(Tests: endurance leadership, motivational sustainability, expectation recalibration)

**Stem 4.9**
"Tell me about a time when you had to simultaneously manage the demands of day-to-day operations while implementing a major change initiative. How did you balance both, and what trade-offs did you make?"
(Tests: dual-operating-system management, transition workload balancing, prioritization under change)

**Stem 4.10**
"Give me an example of when you sought input from people outside your immediate team or function to solve a problem or generate a new approach. Who did you engage, and how did their perspective change your thinking?"
(Tests: boundary-spanning innovation, intellectual humility, diverse input integration)

### 5. Building Relationships and Influence

These stems assess the candidate's ability to build productive relationships across organizational boundaries, influence stakeholders without formal authority, and navigate complex political dynamics. At JL3 and JL4, the focus includes board-level and external stakeholder engagement.

**Stem 5.1**
"Tell me about a specific situation in the last year where you needed to influence a decision but had no formal authority over the people involved. What approach did you take, and what was the outcome?"
(Tests: influence without authority, persuasion strategy, stakeholder mapping)

**Stem 5.2**
"Walk me through a time when you had to build a productive working relationship with someone whose working style, communication preferences, or professional perspective was very different from your own."
(Tests: interpersonal adaptability, perspective-taking, relationship-building across differences)

**Stem 5.3**
"Give me an example of a negotiation you conducted in the past 18 months, whether internal or external, where the stakes were significant. What was your approach, and how did you reach resolution?"
(Tests: negotiation strategy, value creation in disagreement, outcome orientation)

**Stem 5.4**
"Tell me about a time when you had to manage a critical stakeholder who was disengaged, resistant, or actively working against an initiative you were leading. How did you diagnose the situation, and what did you do?"
(Tests: difficult stakeholder management, political acumen, conflict resolution at senior levels)

**Stem 5.5**
"Describe a situation where you collaborated with a team or function that had objectives that were partially or fully in tension with your own. How did you find common ground?"
(Tests: cross-functional collaboration, competing priority navigation, integrative problem-solving)

**Stem 5.6**
"Walk me through a time when you had to represent your organization or function to an external partner, customer, or regulatory body. How did you prepare, and what was the outcome?"
(Tests: external representation, communication under scrutiny, organizational ambassadorship)

**Stem 5.7**
"Give me an example of when you proactively invested time in building a relationship that did not have an immediate transactional purpose but later proved valuable. What prompted you to invest, and how did it pay off?"
(Tests: strategic relationship investment, network cultivation, long-term relational thinking)

**Stem 5.8**
"Tell me about a time when you received feedback that your communication style or approach was not landing effectively with a particular audience. What did you learn, and what did you change?"
(Tests: communication adaptability, receptivity to feedback, self-correction)

**Stem 5.9**
"Describe a specific instance where you had to deliver an unpopular message to a senior leader or executive. How did you frame the message, and how was it received?"
(Tests: upward communication courage, message framing, executive presence under pressure)

**Stem 5.10**
"Walk me through a cross-functional project where alignment broke down partway through. What caused the breakdown, what was your role in restoring alignment, and what did you learn about collaboration?"
(Tests: cross-functional failure recovery, collaborative repair, systemic learning from misalignment)

### 6. Financial Acumen and Business Judgment

These stems elicit evidence of the candidate's ability to manage financial resources, make sound business decisions grounded in economic analysis, and balance short-term financial pressures against long-term value creation. At JL1, stems should reference budget awareness and cost consciousness. At JL3 and JL4, stems should surface P&L ownership, capital allocation, and portfolio-level financial stewardship.

**Stem 6.1**
"Tell me about a specific decision you made in the last year that had a direct, measurable financial impact on your team or business unit. What analysis did you conduct, and what was the result?"
(Tests: financial decision-making, analytical rigor, outcome measurement)

**Stem 6.2**
"Walk me through a time when you had to make a resource allocation decision with a constrained budget. What were the competing demands, and how did you determine where to invest?"
(Tests: resource allocation under constraint, prioritization, cost-benefit reasoning)

**Stem 6.3**
"Give me an example of when you identified a significant cost savings or revenue opportunity that others had not recognized. How did you quantify it, and what action did you take?"
(Tests: financial opportunity identification, business acumen, proactive value creation)

**Stem 6.4**
"Tell me about a situation where you had to justify a significant expenditure or investment to senior leadership. How did you build the business case, and what objections did you face?"
(Tests: business case construction, persuasion with financial data, investment justification)

**Stem 6.5**
"Describe a time when short-term financial pressures conflicted with a longer-term strategic investment you believed was necessary. How did you navigate that tension, and what was the outcome?"
(Tests: short-term vs. long-term trade-off management, strategic financial patience, value horizon thinking)

**Stem 6.6**
"Walk me through a time when you inherited or discovered a financial problem, such as a budget overrun, unaccounted expense, or revenue shortfall, and had to take corrective action."
(Tests: financial problem diagnosis, corrective action, fiscal accountability)

**Stem 6.7**
"Give me a concrete example of when you used financial data or key performance indicators to change the direction of a project or operational decision."
(Tests: data-driven financial decision-making, KPI utilization, course correction based on metrics)

**Stem 6.8**
"Tell me about a time when you had to communicate financial performance, whether positive or negative, to a non-financial audience. How did you make the information accessible without oversimplifying?"
(Tests: financial communication, audience calibration, complex-to-clear translation)

**Stem 6.9**
"Describe a situation where you evaluated a vendor, supplier, or partner proposal. What criteria did you use, how did you assess the financial terms, and what was your recommendation?"
(Tests: vendor/partner financial evaluation, procurement judgment, total cost of ownership analysis)

**Stem 6.10**
"Walk me through a time when you had to say no to a project, initiative, or request because the financial return did not justify the investment. How did you communicate your decision?"
(Tests: financial discipline, kill-decision making, transparent communication of financial rationale)

**Stem 7.1**
"Tell me about a specific skill or capability you deliberately worked to develop in the past 12 months. What prompted you to focus on it, what steps did you take, and how do you know you have improved?"

**Stem 7.2**
"Walk me through a time when you coached a direct report or colleague through a significant professional challenge. What was the situation, what was your coaching approach, and what was the outcome for that person?"
(Tests: coaching capability, developmental orientation, individualized support)

"Give me an example of when you built a development plan for someone on your team that went beyond standard training courses. What did the plan include, and how did you hold the individual accountable?"
(Tests: development plan design, creative developmental interventions, accountability in development)

**Stem 7.4**
"Tell me about a situation where you received critical feedback about your own performance or leadership. How did you respond in the moment, and what did you do with the feedback over time?"
(Tests: feedback receptivity, ego management, behavioral change following input)

**Stem 7.5**
"Describe a time when you identified a gap in your team's collective capability and took action to close it. What was the gap, and what approach did you take?"
(Tests: team capability assessment, capability gap closure, systemic development thinking)

**Stem 7.6**

**Stem 7.7**

(Tests: readiness assessment rigor, succession evaluation, transparent talent communication)

### 8. Integrity and Values Alignment

These stems elicit evidence of the candidate's commitment to ethical decision-making, adherence to organizational values, and willingness to prioritize safety, compliance, and doing the right thing over expedient results. At all job levels, these stems assess whether the candidate has a demonstrated behavioral history of acting with integrity when it is costly or uncomfortable to do so.

**Stem 8.1**
"Tell me about a specific time when you faced pressure to cut corners or compromise a standard, whether related to quality, safety, compliance, or ethics, and chose not to. What was the pressure, and what did you do?"
(Tests: ethical resolve under pressure, standards maintenance, moral courage)

**Stem 8.2**
"Walk me through a situation where you observed a colleague or team member behaving in a way that conflicted with your organization's values or policies. What did you do, and what was the outcome?"
(Tests: bystander intervention, values enforcement, willingness to address misconduct)

**Stem 8.3**
"Give me an example of a time when doing the right thing for the long term required you to make a decision that was unpopular or created short-term difficulty for you or your team."
(Tests: long-term ethical orientation, personal cost tolerance, principled decision-making)

**Stem 8.4**
"Tell me about a situation where you made a mistake that affected others. How did you handle the disclosure, and what did you do to address the impact?"
(Tests: personal accountability, transparency, remediation behavior)

**Stem 8.5**
"Describe a time when you had to navigate a situation where the rules or policies were ambiguous and you had to use your own judgment to determine the right course of action."
(Tests: ethical reasoning in ambiguity, judgment in gray areas, principled autonomy)

**Stem 8.6**
"Walk me through a time when you raised a concern about a safety risk, compliance gap, or ethical issue, even when doing so was uncomfortable or potentially career-limiting."
(Tests: speaking up behavior, safety culture contribution, personal risk tolerance for ethical action)

**Stem 8.7**
"Give me an example of when you had access to sensitive or confidential information and had to make a judgment call about how to handle it. What factors did you consider?"
(Tests: confidentiality judgment, information stewardship, trust management)

**Stem 8.8**
"Tell me about a time when you had to make a decision that balanced the interests of multiple stakeholders who would be differently affected. How did you weigh the competing interests, and how did you communicate your rationale?"
(Tests: stakeholder equity reasoning, transparent decision communication, fairness orientation)

**Stem 8.9**
"Describe a situation where organizational pressure or cultural norms pushed in a direction you believed was inconsistent with your values. How did you respond?"
(Tests: values-driven resistance to conformity pressure, cultural counter-normative behavior, integrity under social pressure)

**Stem 8.10**
"Walk me through a time when you ensured that a process, policy, or decision was fair and equitable for all people involved, particularly when it would have been easier to apply a less rigorous standard."
(Tests: equity commitment in practice, procedural fairness enforcement, inclusion-driven execution)

## University Recruiting Adaptations

Candidates entering the University Recruiting pipeline (internship, co-op, and early-career JL1 roles) present a distinct assessment challenge: they possess limited professional behavioral history. The following principles and modifications ensure that BEI stems remain valid and fair for this population while still eliciting scorable behavioral evidence.

### Adaptation Principles

**Replace corporate context with universally accessible contexts.** University candidates draw behavioral evidence from academic coursework, group projects, student organizations, athletic teams, part-time employment, volunteer work, internships, and academic research. Stems must explicitly signal that these contexts are valid sources of behavioral evidence.

**Extend the temporal anchor.** Use "during your academic program," "within the last two to three years," or "during a recent internship or significant project" rather than "in the last 12 months," which may yield an insufficiently narrow window of experience.

**Reduce the expected scope of impact.** Do not require evidence of enterprise-level outcomes, P&L management, or multi-team leadership. Instead, focus on the foundational behavioral indicators that predict future capability: initiative, learning agility, collaboration, accountability, and the ability to navigate novel situations.

**Maintain the same scoring dimensions.** The underlying competency being measured does not change; only the context in which the behavior occurred differs. Assessors must be calibrated to score university candidate responses against level-appropriate behavioral anchors, not against the full JL2-JL4 anchor scale.

### Modified Stem Examples

**Original (JL2+):** "Tell me about a specific time in the last year when you had to give difficult performance feedback to a direct report."

**University Adaptation:** "Tell me about a time during a group project, team activity, or work experience when you had to address a teammate or colleague whose contribution was not meeting the group's expectations. How did you approach the conversation, and what happened?"
(Tests the same construct -- feedback delivery and interpersonal courage -- in an accessible context.)

**Original (JL3+):** "Walk me through a time when you had to develop a plan that spanned more than one fiscal year."

**University Adaptation:** "Walk me through a project, initiative, or goal you pursued that required sustained effort over multiple months or semesters. How did you plan your approach, and how did you stay on track when competing demands arose?"
(Tests the same construct -- long-range planning and sustained execution -- without requiring corporate planning experience.)

**Original (JL2+):** "Give me an example of when you identified a significant cost savings or revenue opportunity."

**University Adaptation:** "Give me an example of a time when you found a way to accomplish a goal more efficiently, whether by saving money, reducing time, or using fewer resources than originally planned. What did you notice, and what did you do?"
(Tests the same construct -- financial/resource acumen -- at an appropriate scope.)

**Original (JL3+):** "Tell me about a time when you led your team through a significant organizational change."

**University Adaptation:** "Tell me about a time when a team or organization you were part of went through a significant change, such as a leadership transition, a shift in project direction, or a major disruption. What was your role during the transition, and how did you contribute?"
(Tests the same construct -- change navigation -- without requiring formal leadership authority.)

**Additional University-Specific Stems**

"Tell me about a time during your academic program when you had to learn something entirely new in a short period of time. What was your approach, and how did you know you had achieved sufficient understanding?"
(Tests: learning agility, self-regulated learning, metacognition)

"Give me an example of a time when you received a grade, evaluation, or outcome that was lower than you expected. What did you do in response?"
(Tests: feedback processing, resilience, corrective action orientation)

"Walk me through a situation where you had to work with a group of people you did not choose, such as in an assigned class project. What challenges arose, and how did you contribute to the group's effectiveness?"
(Tests: collaboration in non-elective teams, adaptability, interpersonal flexibility)

## Stems to Avoid

The following examples illustrate common question construction errors that degrade the psychometric validity of the structured interview. Each example is accompanied by a diagnostic explanation of the specific failure mode. These anti-patterns must be eliminated during guide development and assessor training.

**Anti-Pattern 1: Leading Language**
"Tell me about a time when you demonstrated strong leadership by motivating your team to exceed their goals."

*Why it fails:* The stem embeds both the competency label ("strong leadership") and the desired outcome ("motivating your team to exceed their goals") within the question. This telegraphs the socially desirable response and invites self-serving narrative construction. The candidate knows exactly what the interviewer wants to hear, eliminating the diagnostic value of the question.

**Anti-Pattern 2: Hypothetical Framing**
"If you were assigned to lead a cross-functional team with members from five different countries, how would you approach building trust?"

*Why it fails:* This is a situational question, not a behavioral event question. It measures behavioral intention and self-presentation, not demonstrated behavioral history. Candidates can construct idealized answers that bear no resemblance to their actual behavior. Behavioral stems must anchor to past events.

**Anti-Pattern 3: Trait-Based Question**
"Would you describe yourself as someone who is detail-oriented? Can you give me an example?"

*Why it fails:* The opening question is a self-report trait assessment disguised as an interview question. It invites a binary affirmation followed by a cherry-picked anecdote. The behavioral evidence is secondary to the self-labeling, which has negligible predictive validity for job performance.

**Anti-Pattern 4: Double-Barreled Stem**
"Tell me about a time when you had to manage a difficult customer relationship while also dealing with a major internal reorganization."

*Why it fails:* The stem demands two simultaneous behavioral episodes, making it nearly impossible for the candidate to provide a coherent STAR narrative. If the candidate did experience both simultaneously, the response will be superficial on both dimensions. If they did not, they must fabricate a connection. Each competency dimension requires its own discrete stem.

**Anti-Pattern 5: Vague, Unanchored Stem**
"Tell me about a challenge you've faced at work."

*Why it fails:* The stem provides no temporal anchor, no contextual frame, and no behavioral specificity. The candidate can select any experience from their entire career, making cross-candidate comparison meaningless. The term "challenge" is so broad that the resulting behavioral evidence cannot be reliably mapped to any single competency dimension.

**Anti-Pattern 6: Culturally Biased Idiom**
"Tell me about a time you had to roll with the punches when a project went sideways."

*Why it fails:* The idioms "roll with the punches" and "went sideways" are culturally specific English expressions that may be unfamiliar or confusing to non-native speakers, introducing construct-irrelevant variance. In a global assessment context, the stem must use plain, universally accessible language.

**Anti-Pattern 7: Closed-Ended Behavioral**
"Did you ever have to make a tough decision about letting someone go?"

*Why it fails:* The yes/no construction permits a one-word response and fails to prompt narrative elaboration. Even if followed by "Tell me about it," the initial closed framing signals to the candidate that a brief response is acceptable. Stems must be open-ended from the first word.

**Anti-Pattern 8: Recency Bias Inducer**
"What is the most impressive thing you have ever accomplished in your career?"

*Why it fails:* The superlative framing ("most impressive") invites the candidate to select their single best self-presentation narrative rather than a representative behavioral sample. It also introduces recency and salience biases, as candidates disproportionately select recent or emotionally vivid experiences regardless of their relevance to the target competency.

**Anti-Pattern 9: Theoretical/Knowledge-Based**
"What do you think makes an effective leader?"

*Why it fails:* This is a knowledge or opinion question, not a behavioral question. It assesses the candidate's declarative knowledge about leadership theory, not their demonstrated leadership behavior. A candidate may articulate a sophisticated leadership philosophy while exhibiting none of those behaviors in practice.

**Anti-Pattern 10: Multiple Competency Contamination**
"Tell me about a time you showed integrity, built trust with your team, and delivered a tough project on time."

*Why it fails:* This stem attempts to assess three distinct competency dimensions (integrity, trust-building, and execution) within a single question. The candidate cannot adequately address all three in a single response, and the assessor cannot reliably assign a competency-specific score. Each competency requires an independent behavioral episode and scoring event.

**Anti-Pattern 11: Presuppositional Stem**
"Tell me about the most recent conflict you had with your manager."

*Why it fails:* The stem presupposes that the candidate has had conflict with their manager, which may not be true. If the candidate has not experienced this specific situation, they are forced to either fabricate or awkwardly decline, creating unnecessary interview friction. Stems should use conditional language: "Tell me about a time, if applicable, when..."

**Anti-Pattern 12: Social Desirability Trap**
"Tell me about a time when you went above and beyond for a customer or colleague."

*Why it fails:* The phrase "above and beyond" is a social desirability cue that signals the interviewer values discretionary effort. Every candidate will present a self-flattering narrative. The stem fails to create conditions under which differential behavioral quality can emerge because the framing precludes any response that is not self-enhancing.
