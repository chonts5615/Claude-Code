## Why Behavioral Anchors Matter

Unanchored numeric scales produce unreliable ratings. When interviewers are handed a 1-5 scale with no behavioral descriptions, each rater constructs a private, idiosyncratic definition of what constitutes a "3" versus a "4." The result is low inter-rater agreement, inconsistent candidate evaluations, and selection decisions that reflect interviewer subjectivity rather than candidate behavior. In a global enterprise operating across dozens of countries with hundreds of trained interviewers, this subjectivity compounds into systemic measurement error that degrades the predictive validity of the entire interview process.

Behavioral anchors solve this problem by creating a shared frame of reference. When every rater reads the same concrete behavioral description for each scale point, the ambiguity collapses. Raters are no longer scoring against their personal mental model of "good performance"; they are matching observed candidate behavior to a standardized behavioral benchmark derived from job analysis data.

The meta-analytic evidence is unambiguous. Research synthesizing decades of structured interview studies demonstrates that behaviorally anchored rating scales improve inter-rater reliability, as measured by ICC(2,k), by approximately .15 to .20 over unanchored numeric scales. This magnitude of improvement translates directly into higher criterion-related validity for the interview as a selection instrument, which in turn translates into measurably better hiring outcomes at the enterprise level.

From a legal defensibility perspective, anchored scales demonstrate job-relatedness in a manner that unanchored scales cannot. In the event of an adverse impact challenge, the organization must demonstrate that each component of the selection process is grounded in a systematic job analysis and that scoring criteria are directly linked to the competencies required for successful job performance. Behavioral anchors provide this evidentiary chain. An unanchored scale that simply asks an interviewer to rate "leadership" on a 1-5 continuum provides no such defensibility. Anchored scales that describe specific, observable, job-relevant behaviors at each scale point establish the requisite linkage between the selection tool and the job requirements, consistent with the SIOP Principles for the Validation and Use of Personnel Selection Procedures and the APA Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing.

## The 1-5 Rating Scale Architecture

### Scale Point Definitions (Universal Framework)

| Rating | Label | Definition |
|--------|-------|------------|
| 1 | **Developing** | Behavior absent, counterproductive, or significantly below the threshold for the target level. The candidate either cannot articulate the competency in action or describes behaviors that would actively undermine performance at the target job level. |
| 2 | **Emerging** | Behavior partially present but inconsistent, with notable gaps. The candidate demonstrates awareness of the competency and provides some relevant behavioral evidence, but the examples lack depth, scope, or consistency required for the target level. |
| 3 | **Proficient** | Behavior consistently demonstrated at the expected level for the role; meets expectations. The candidate provides clear, specific behavioral evidence that matches the scope, complexity, and impact expected at the target job level. This is the benchmark rating and should represent the majority of successful hires. |
| 4 | **Advanced** | Behavior exceeds expectations; demonstrates sophistication, breadth, or impact beyond the role requirement. The candidate provides behavioral evidence indicating they operate above the target level in this competency, whether through broader scope, more complex stakeholder navigation, or more sophisticated strategic reasoning. |
| 5 | **Exemplary** | Behavior is a clear organizational strength; the candidate could coach others and represents best-in-class for this level. The candidate provides behavioral evidence of extraordinary impact, innovation, or influence that would position them as a role model or internal subject matter expert in this competency area. |

### Minimum Anchor Requirements

Anchors MUST be written for levels 1, 3, and 5 at a minimum. These three anchor points establish the lower bound, the expected standard, and the aspirational ceiling. This triad is the minimum threshold for defensibility and inter-rater calibration.

Anchors SHOULD also be written for levels 2 and 4 when feasible. The inclusion of all five anchor points meaningfully improves inter-rater agreement by eliminating the ambiguity that arises when raters must mentally interpolate between non-adjacent anchors. For high-stakes assessments such as the SM1 Leadership Assessment or any selection decision involving executive-level roles, all five anchor points are required, not optional.

Every anchor must describe observable behavior, not traits or intentions. An anchor is not a personality descriptor; it is a behavioral exemplar that an interviewer can match against a candidate's reported actions. The test for any anchor is straightforward: could two independent raters read this anchor and agree on whether a given candidate response does or does not match it? If the answer is uncertain, the anchor requires revision.

## Anchor Writing Rules

### DO

Use action verbs that describe what the candidate did, not what they are. Effective anchor language relies on verbs such as identifies, develops, communicates, allocates, designs, implements, delegates, reforecasts, escalates, coaches, restructures, and facilitates. These verbs force the anchor into behavioral territory and away from trait attribution.

Specify scope and impact to differentiate between job levels. The same competency manifests differently at JL1 versus JL4. The critical differentiator is frequently the scope of the behavior: "within the team" versus "across the function" versus "across the enterprise." Anchors must make this scope explicit so that raters can accurately calibrate the candidate's behavioral evidence to the target job level rather than scoring against an abstract, level-agnostic standard.

Include frequency and consistency markers that signal whether the behavior is habitual or episodic. Language such as "consistently," "proactively," "when prompted," "routinely," and "in isolated instances" provides raters with the vocabulary to distinguish between a candidate who demonstrated a behavior once under ideal conditions and a candidate who demonstrates it reliably across contexts.

Reference stakeholder interactions where the competency involves influence, collaboration, or communication. Specifying the stakeholder level (direct reports, peers, cross-functional partners, senior leadership, external vendors) clarifies the complexity and political sophistication required at each anchor level.

Differentiate by quality of behavior, not just quantity. The difference between a 3 and a 4 is not that the candidate "did more of it." The difference is that the candidate demonstrated greater sophistication, broader contextual awareness, more proactive initiative, or higher-order strategic reasoning in how they executed the behavior.

### DO NOT

Use trait adjectives as anchors. Descriptors such as "strategic," "collaborative," "innovative," or "resilient" are not behaviors. They are inferences that a rater draws from observed behaviors. An anchor that reads "demonstrates strategic thinking" tells the rater nothing about what behavioral evidence to look for; it simply relocates the subjectivity problem from the numeric scale to the anchor text.

Write anchors that differ only by adverbs. If the only difference between a level 3 and a level 4 anchor is the insertion of "effectively" or "very effectively," the anchors are functionally identical and will not improve inter-rater agreement. Each anchor level must describe qualitatively distinct behavioral patterns.

Include multiple independent behaviors in a single anchor (double-barreled anchors). An anchor that reads "delegates tasks effectively and communicates transparently with senior leadership" conflates two separate competency dimensions. A candidate who excels at delegation but struggles with upward communication cannot be accurately scored. Each anchor should describe a coherent behavioral pattern within a single competency dimension.

Use absolute language that makes accurate rating impossible. Terms such as "always" and "never" create anchors that no human candidate can realistically meet or fail. Replace absolutes with calibrated frequency markers: "consistently," "rarely," "in the majority of situations."

Write anchors so vague that any response could plausibly be scored there. An anchor that reads "handles challenges appropriately" provides zero discriminative value. The rater cannot determine whether a candidate's response matches this description because the description matches virtually everything.

## Exemplar Anchors by Competency Category

### Example 1: Leading Through Financial Constraints (JL4 / SM1)

This competency is core to the Cargill Leadership Academy (CLA) curriculum for SM1 leaders operating at the Vice President equivalent level. The behavioral anchors reflect the expectation that SM1 leaders manage multi-million-dollar portfolios, make enterprise-level tradeoff decisions, and communicate financial rationale to the C-suite and Board-level stakeholders.

| Rating | Anchor |
|--------|--------|
| **5 - Exemplary** | Proactively reforecasts based on leading indicators before variances materialize, reallocates capital across the portfolio to preserve strategic priorities, and makes documented tradeoff decisions with explicit risk quantification. Communicates tradeoffs transparently to senior stakeholders with data-backed rationale, including scenario modeling of alternative allocation strategies. Other leaders seek this individual's counsel when navigating their own financial constraints. |
| **4 - Advanced** | Manages budget with foresight, identifies emerging variances before they escalate using trend analysis and operational signals, and proposes reallocation options with clear impact analysis. Engages cross-functional partners early to negotiate shared resource adjustments rather than making unilateral cuts. |
| **3 - Proficient** | Manages to budget with discipline, makes reasonable tradeoffs when resource constraints emerge, and communicates variances to leadership with appropriate context and a recommended path forward. Demonstrates understanding of how budget decisions affect downstream operational commitments. |
| **2 - Emerging** | Reacts to budget pressure after variances have already emerged, proposes cost reductions without strategic prioritization or impact assessment, and escalates tradeoff decisions upward without a recommendation or options analysis. Financial communication to stakeholders lacks specificity. |
| **1 - Developing** | Treats budget as a fixed, non-negotiable constraint rather than a strategic resource allocation tool. Surprises stakeholders with variances that should have been foreseeable. Cannot articulate the tradeoff rationale behind resource decisions or defaults to across-the-board cuts without regard for strategic priorities. |

### Example 2: Building Team Capability (JL2 / Manager)

This competency targets JL2 managers who directly lead teams of individual contributors. The behavioral focus is on developing the skills and careers of direct reports through deliberate coaching, stretch assignments, and feedback practices.

| Rating | Anchor |
|--------|--------|
| **5 - Exemplary** | Builds a systematic development culture within the team by maintaining individualized development plans for each direct report, calibrating stretch assignments to each person's growth edge, and providing real-time coaching feedback tied to observable behaviors. Tracks development progress quarterly and adjusts plans based on performance data. Team members consistently report high developmental support in engagement surveys, and at least one direct report has been promoted or placed into a broader role within the past 18 months. |
| **4 - Advanced** | Creates meaningful development plans for direct reports that include specific skill targets and stretch assignments. Delivers coaching feedback that references specific behaviors and their impact, not just outcomes. Proactively identifies high-potential team members and advocates for their visibility with senior leadership. |
| **3 - Proficient** | Conducts regular one-on-one meetings with direct reports that include development discussions. Assigns stretch tasks that align with stated development goals. Provides constructive feedback on a consistent basis, citing specific examples. Supports direct reports in pursuing learning opportunities and removes administrative barriers to their development. |
| **2 - Emerging** | Holds development conversations primarily during annual review cycles rather than as an ongoing practice. Assigns stretch tasks but does not connect them to a coherent development plan. Feedback tends to focus on outcomes ("the project went well") rather than on the specific behaviors that drove the outcome. |
| **1 - Developing** | Does not conduct development conversations beyond mandatory annual reviews. Assigns work based solely on current capability rather than developmental need. Feedback is absent, purely evaluative ("meets expectations"), or delivered only when performance problems arise. Team members report feeling unsupported in their career growth. |

### Example 3: Driving Cross-Functional Collaboration (JL3 / Senior Manager)

This competency targets JL3 senior managers who must operate across functional and geographic boundaries to deliver enterprise-level outcomes. The behavioral focus is on building influence without direct authority, navigating competing priorities, and creating durable collaborative structures.

| Rating | Anchor |
|--------|--------|
| **5 - Exemplary** | Designs and institutionalizes cross-functional governance structures (e.g., joint steering committees, shared KPI dashboards, co-owned project charters) that outlast any single initiative. Proactively identifies misaligned incentives across functions and escalates structural solutions to senior leadership. Is sought out by peers across the enterprise as the convener of choice for complex, multi-stakeholder initiatives. Resolves cross-functional conflicts by reframing disagreements around shared enterprise objectives rather than functional interests. |
| **4 - Advanced** | Initiates cross-functional partnerships before being asked, identifies mutual dependencies early, and negotiates resource-sharing agreements that account for each function's constraints. Invests time in understanding partner functions' priorities and adapts communication and proposals to align with their strategic language. Surfaces and addresses misalignment before it becomes a blocker. |
| **3 - Proficient** | Collaborates effectively with cross-functional partners when projects require it. Establishes clear roles, responsibilities, and decision rights at the outset of joint initiatives. Communicates progress and blockers to all relevant stakeholders on a regular cadence. Compromises when functional priorities conflict, arriving at workable solutions that preserve project timelines. |
| **2 - Emerging** | Engages cross-functional partners when directed but does not proactively seek collaboration opportunities. Collaboration tends to be transactional rather than relational, with limited investment in understanding partner functions' constraints or priorities. When cross-functional conflicts arise, defaults to escalation rather than direct resolution. |
| **1 - Developing** | Operates in functional silos and resists or avoids cross-functional engagement. Does not establish shared goals or governance mechanisms for joint work. Blames partner functions when collaborative initiatives fail without examining own contribution to the breakdown. Communication with cross-functional peers is infrequent, reactive, and typically adversarial. |

### Example 4: Demonstrating Learning Agility (University Recruiting)

This competency is designed for early-career candidates with limited professional experience. Behavioral anchors are calibrated to draw on academic, extracurricular, internship, and personal experiences rather than requiring multi-year corporate examples. The focus is on the candidate's capacity to learn from novel situations, apply lessons across contexts, and self-correct based on feedback.

| Rating | Anchor |
|--------|--------|
| **5 - Exemplary** | Describes multiple instances of entering unfamiliar domains (academic, extracurricular, or internship) and achieving measurable outcomes within a compressed timeline. Articulates a deliberate personal learning process (e.g., identifying knowledge gaps, seeking out experts, testing approaches, iterating based on results). Provides a specific example of receiving critical feedback, describing how they changed their approach and what the improved outcome was. Demonstrates the ability to extract transferable principles from one experience and apply them to a structurally different challenge. |
| **4 - Advanced** | Describes a situation requiring rapid learning with specificity about the steps taken to acquire new knowledge or skills. Demonstrates self-awareness about what made the situation challenging and what they would do differently in retrospect. Provides a concrete example of applying a lesson learned in one context to a different situation with a positive result. |
| **3 - Proficient** | Describes a specific situation where they had to learn something new to accomplish a goal. Explains the steps they took to learn (e.g., research, asking questions, practice) and connects those steps to the outcome achieved. Demonstrates openness to feedback by describing a time they received constructive input and adjusted their behavior accordingly. |
| **2 - Emerging** | Describes a learning experience but remains vague about the specific actions taken to acquire the new skill or knowledge. When asked about feedback, acknowledges receiving it but cannot articulate how it changed their subsequent behavior. Struggles to connect lessons from one experience to a different context when probed. |
| **1 - Developing** | Cannot identify a specific example of learning something new in a challenging situation, or provides an example that lacks behavioral detail. When asked about feedback, deflects, minimizes, or describes disagreeing with the feedback without demonstrating reflection. Shows no evidence of transferring lessons across contexts. |

## Level-Shifted Anchors: Same Competency, Different Expectations

The following table demonstrates how behavioral anchors for the competency "Strategic Thinking" shift across job levels at the Level 3 (Proficient) anchor point. This illustrates the principle that the same competency does not require the same behavior at every level; the scope, complexity, time horizon, and stakeholder altitude of the expected behavior increases systematically with job level.

| Job Level | Level 3 (Proficient) Anchor for Strategic Thinking |
|-----------|-----------------------------------------------------|
| **JL1 (Individual Contributor)** | Understands how own work connects to team objectives. Can articulate how daily tasks and project deliverables support the broader goals of the immediate team. When priorities shift, adjusts own work plan accordingly without requiring detailed direction on every task. |
| **JL2 (Manager)** | Translates departmental strategy into team-level quarterly plans with clear milestones and resource requirements. Adjusts team priorities when business conditions change, communicating the rationale to direct reports. Connects team performance metrics to departmental KPIs. |
| **JL3 (Senior Manager)** | Develops functional strategy aligned to enterprise goals. Makes cross-functional tradeoff decisions with two- to three-year time horizons, balancing near-term operational demands against longer-term capability investments. Engages peer leaders to ensure functional strategies are mutually reinforcing rather than conflicting. |
| **JL4 / SM1 (Vice President equivalent)** | Shapes enterprise strategy by integrating competitive intelligence, regulatory trends, and internal capability assessments into long-range market strategies. Evaluates and communicates strategic scenarios to the executive leadership team, recommending portfolio-level resource allocation decisions that position the organization for sustained competitive advantage over a five- to ten-year horizon. |

When building interview guides, the anchor generator must identify the target job level and calibrate the scope, stakeholder altitude, time horizon, and decision complexity of each anchor accordingly. A JL1 anchor that references "enterprise strategy" or a JL4 anchor that references "daily task management" signals a miscalibrated rubric that will produce inaccurate ratings.

## Common Anchor Failures

The following table catalogs frequently observed anchor writing errors with corrected alternatives. These patterns should be treated as a diagnostic checklist during quality review of any newly generated BARS rubric.

| # | Failure Type | Bad Anchor Example | Why It Fails | Corrected Anchor |
|---|-------------|-------------------|--------------|-----------------|
| 1 | **Trait substitution** | "Shows leadership." | "Leadership" is a trait inference, not an observable behavior. Two raters will disagree on what behavioral evidence constitutes "showing" it. | "Delegates tasks based on team members' developmental needs, provides clear success criteria, and follows up with coaching feedback within 48 hours." |
| 2 | **Adverb-only differentiation** | Level 3: "Manages stakeholders effectively." Level 4: "Manages stakeholders very effectively." | The only difference is an adverb intensifier; the anchors describe the same behavior and provide no discriminative value to the rater. | Level 3: "Communicates project status to stakeholders on a regular cadence and addresses their concerns within agreed timelines." Level 4: "Anticipates stakeholder concerns before they are raised, proactively provides data to address potential objections, and adapts communication style to each stakeholder's decision-making preferences." |
| 3 | **Double-barreled anchor** | "Builds strong relationships with clients and delivers projects on time and under budget." | Conflates relationship management and project execution. A candidate who excels at one and struggles at the other cannot be accurately scored. | Split into two separate competency dimensions, each with its own anchor set. If a single anchor is required, focus on one dimension: "Builds and maintains client relationships by conducting regular check-ins, soliciting feedback, and adjusting deliverables based on evolving client needs." |
| 4 | **Absolute language** | "Always meets deadlines without exception." | No human being always meets every deadline. The anchor is unscorable because no candidate can achieve it, rendering the scale point functionally unusable. | "Consistently meets deadlines across concurrent projects. When a deadline is at risk, communicates the delay early and proposes a revised timeline with mitigation steps." |
| 5 | **Vague catch-all** | "Handles challenges appropriately." | Every candidate "handles challenges" in some fashion. The anchor lacks sufficient specificity to distinguish between a Level 2 and a Level 4 response. | "When facing an unexpected project obstacle, identifies the root cause, evaluates two or more alternative approaches, selects a path forward based on impact and feasibility, and communicates the decision to affected stakeholders." |
| 6 | **Outcome-only anchor** | "Achieves a 95% customer satisfaction rating." | Describes a result, not a behavior. The rater cannot score the candidate's behavioral process from an interview response about an outcome metric. | "Solicits customer feedback systematically after each engagement, analyzes recurring themes, and implements process changes that address the top two sources of dissatisfaction." |
| 7 | **Level-misaligned scope** | JL1 anchor: "Develops enterprise-wide talent strategy and presents to the Board of Directors." | The scope of the behavior is wildly misaligned with the job level. No individual contributor develops enterprise strategy or presents to Boards. This anchor will produce floor effects where every JL1 candidate receives a 1. | JL1 anchor: "Identifies opportunities to improve team-level processes and proposes changes to their direct manager with supporting rationale." |
| 8 | **Jargon-dependent anchor** | "Leverages synergistic cross-pollination of ideation frameworks to drive paradigmatic innovation." | The anchor is unintelligible. Raters cannot match candidate behavior to language they do not understand, and the anchor conceals the absence of a real behavioral description behind corporate jargon. | "Gathers input from team members across different project areas, identifies overlapping ideas, and proposes a combined approach that addresses multiple workstreams simultaneously." |
| 9 | **Negation-only low anchor** | Level 1: "Does not demonstrate the competency." | This is a non-anchor. It tells the rater nothing about what a Level 1 response actually sounds like. Raters need to know what deficient behavior looks like, not merely that the target behavior is absent. | Level 1: "Cannot provide a specific example when asked. When pressed, describes a situation where they deferred all decisions to their manager and took no independent action to address the problem." |
| 10 | **Inconsistent construct across levels** | Level 3: "Communicates project updates clearly." Level 5: "Develops innovative machine learning models for supply chain optimization." | The anchors describe entirely different competencies at different scale points. Level 3 addresses communication; Level 5 addresses technical innovation. The construct being measured shifts across the scale, destroying internal consistency. | Ensure all five anchor levels describe progressive sophistication within the same behavioral construct. Level 3: "Communicates project updates clearly to all stakeholders on a regular cadence." Level 5: "Tailors communication strategy to each stakeholder group, uses data visualization to make complex information accessible, and proactively frames updates within the context of enterprise strategic priorities." |

## Calibration Notes

When two raters disagree by two or more scale points on the same candidate response, the most probable cause is ambiguous anchor language, not rater incompetence. A two-point disagreement signals that the anchors permit multiple valid interpretations of the same behavioral evidence. The corrective action is to revise the anchor text, not to retrain the raters. Revisions should focus on adding behavioral specificity, clarifying scope indicators, and eliminating language that could reasonably map to multiple scale points.

Anchor calibration exercises should use recorded mock interview responses, not hypothetical candidate descriptions. When calibration sessions rely on facilitators verbally describing what a candidate "might say," each participant constructs a different mental representation of the response. Recorded responses (video or audio, with transcripts) ensure that all raters are evaluating identical behavioral evidence, which isolates disagreements attributable to anchor ambiguity from disagreements attributable to stimulus variation.

Document edge cases that emerge during calibration as supplementary anchor notes. In every calibration session, raters will encounter candidate responses that fall between anchor points or that demonstrate the competency in an unexpected but valid manner. These edge cases should be captured in writing and appended to the rubric as "anchor supplements." Over successive calibration rounds, these supplements accumulate into a rich case library that further tightens inter-rater agreement without requiring wholesale anchor rewrites.

Calibration sessions should include a minimum of three raters scoring the same set of five to eight recorded responses independently before group discussion. Calculate the ICC(2,k) across raters for each competency dimension. If any competency dimension produces an ICC below .60, the anchors for that dimension require immediate revision before the rubric is deployed in live interviews.

For the SM1 Leadership Assessment and the Cargill Leadership Academy, calibration must be conducted annually using a refreshed set of recorded responses that reflect the current behavioral expectations of the role. Anchors that were valid in a prior year may become misaligned if the organization's strategic priorities, leadership competency model, or operating context has materially changed.

All calibration documentation, including ICC calculations, anchor revision logs, edge case supplements, and rater participation records, must be retained as part of the assessment program's validation evidence file. This documentation is essential for demonstrating the ongoing psychometric integrity of the selection process in the event of an internal audit or external legal challenge.
