## The Level Differentiation Problem

Most competency frameworks fail because they recycle the same behavioral indicators across levels with minor adverb changes ("manages effectively" becomes "manages very effectively"). This produces interviews that cannot discriminate between a strong JL2 and a developing JL3. The consequence is that organizations promote based on tenure or political capital rather than demonstrated behavioral readiness for the next level of complexity.

Differentiation must be qualitative, not quantitative. A JL3 candidate is not simply "doing more" of what a JL2 does; they are operating in a fundamentally different problem space characterized by longer time horizons, greater ambiguity, more complex stakeholder networks, and tradeoffs that require optimizing across organizational boundaries rather than within a single team. Interview content must reflect these qualitative shifts in the nature of work, not merely scale up the same behavioral anchors with intensifying adverbs.

The five job levels addressed in this framework are:

| Level | Role Equivalent | Core Characterization |
|-------|----------------|----------------------|
| **UR** | University Recruiting / Entry-Level | Limited professional experience; assessed on potential indicators rather than demonstrated organizational impact |
| **JL1** | Individual Contributor | Owns individual work products within established processes; accountable for personal output and immediate team contributions |
| **JL2** | Manager | Manages people or significant projects; accountable for team-level results and direct report development |
| **JL3** | Senior Manager | Manages managers; cross-functional scope; accountable for function-level outcomes and organizational capability building |
| **JL4** | Director / VP-Equivalent (SM1) | Enterprise scope with P&L responsibility; accountable for business unit or enterprise results, market positioning, and portfolio-level resource allocation |

## Five Dimensions of Level Shift

The following five dimensions represent the primary axes along which the nature of work shifts as individuals progress through the leadership pipeline. When generating interview content, every question, probe, and rubric anchor should be calibrated against these dimensions to ensure that the behavioral evidence solicited is appropriate for the target level.

### 1. Time Horizon

Time horizon refers to the planning window within which the individual is expected to operate and the temporal scope of the outcomes for which they are held accountable.

| Level | Time Horizon | Operational Implication |
|-------|-------------|------------------------|
| **UR** | Current semester or project lifecycle (weeks to months) | Candidates are managing immediate deadlines and short-term deliverables. Questions should anchor to discrete academic projects, internship rotations, or extracurricular commitments with clear start and end dates. |
| **JL1** | Current quarter to current year | The individual plans and executes within established quarterly or annual cycles. Behavioral evidence should reflect the ability to sustain effort across multiple months, track progress against defined milestones, and adjust tactics within a given planning period. |
| **JL2** | Current year to 18 months | Managers must anticipate team needs beyond the current quarter, including talent development cycles, resource planning, and initiative sequencing. Questions should probe for evidence of forward-looking planning that extends beyond immediate deliverables. |
| **JL3** | 1-3 year planning horizon | Senior managers set multi-year functional strategies, build organizational capabilities that take years to mature, and sequence investments across planning cycles. Behavioral evidence should demonstrate the ability to hold competing time horizons simultaneously and make near-term sacrifices for long-term positioning. |
| **JL4** | 3-5+ year strategic horizon; shapes the planning framework itself | At the enterprise level, the individual does not merely plan within the existing strategic framework but actively shapes and redefines it. Behavioral evidence should demonstrate the ability to anticipate market shifts, position the organization for future competitive landscapes, and create the planning architecture within which subordinate leaders operate. |

### 2. Scope of Impact

Scope of impact describes the breadth and depth of organizational outcomes for which the individual is accountable.

| Level | Scope of Impact | Operational Implication |
|-------|----------------|------------------------|
| **UR** | Individual deliverables, small team contributions | The candidate's impact is limited to their own work product and their contribution to small group outcomes. Behavioral evidence should focus on personal accountability and the ability to contribute constructively within a team context. |
| **JL1** | Own work product, immediate team outcomes | The individual contributor owns specific outputs and influences team results through the quality and timeliness of their contributions. Questions should probe for evidence of ownership, technical proficiency, and collaborative impact. |
| **JL2** | Team-level results, direct report development | The manager is accountable not only for collective team output but for the growth and capability development of individual team members. Behavioral evidence should demonstrate the ability to drive results through others and invest in talent development simultaneously. |
| **JL3** | Function-level or multi-team results, organizational capability | The senior manager's impact extends beyond any single team to encompass entire functional areas or multi-team ecosystems. Questions should probe for evidence of building organizational capabilities that outlast the leader's direct involvement. |
| **JL4** | Business unit or enterprise results, market positioning, P&L outcomes | The executive is accountable for measurable financial and strategic outcomes at the business unit or enterprise level. Behavioral evidence should demonstrate the ability to translate strategic intent into operational reality across complex organizational structures. |

### 3. Stakeholder Complexity

Stakeholder complexity refers to the diversity, seniority, and competing interests of the constituencies the individual must navigate.

| Level | Key Stakeholders | Operational Implication |
|-------|-----------------|------------------------|
| **UR** | Peers, professors, project supervisors | Stakeholder relationships are relatively straightforward, with clear authority structures. Questions should explore how the candidate navigates feedback, collaborates with peers, and manages upward communication. |
| **JL1** | Direct manager, immediate teammates, internal customers | The individual contributor manages a small set of stakeholders with relatively aligned interests. Behavioral evidence should focus on responsiveness, proactive communication, and the ability to manage expectations. |
| **JL2** | Direct reports, skip-level stakeholders, peer managers | The manager navigates both downward (team) and lateral (peer) relationships, with occasional upward visibility. Questions should probe for evidence of managing competing needs across these stakeholder groups. |
| **JL3** | Senior leadership, external partners, cross-functional executives | The senior manager must influence without direct authority across organizational boundaries and manage relationships with senior leaders who hold competing strategic priorities. Behavioral evidence should demonstrate political acumen, coalition building, and the ability to represent functional interests at the leadership table. |
| **JL4** | C-suite, board, regulators, major external partners, industry bodies | The executive navigates the most complex stakeholder environment, including governance bodies, regulatory agencies, and external partners whose interests may be fundamentally misaligned. Questions should probe for evidence of managing institutional-level relationships and balancing fiduciary obligations against operational realities. |

### 4. Ambiguity Tolerance

Ambiguity tolerance describes the degree to which the individual must operate effectively without clear direction, complete information, or established precedent.

| Level | Ambiguity Profile | Operational Implication |
|-------|-------------------|------------------------|
| **UR** | Execute defined tasks with guidance; demonstrate willingness to ask questions | Candidates are expected to follow established processes and seek clarification proactively. Behavioral evidence should focus on intellectual curiosity, comfort with uncertainty, and the initiative to resolve ambiguity rather than freeze. |
| **JL1** | Execute established processes; adapt when given direction | The individual contributor works within known frameworks and adapts when supervisory guidance signals a shift. Questions should probe for evidence of flexibility and the ability to execute reliably even when conditions change. |
| **JL2** | Adapt known approaches to new situations; make tactical judgment calls | The manager encounters novel situations that require adapting existing playbooks without explicit direction. Behavioral evidence should demonstrate the ability to exercise independent judgment, make defensible decisions with incomplete information, and own the consequences. |
| **JL3** | Design new approaches for ambiguous problems; make strategic tradeoffs with incomplete data | The senior manager regularly confronts problems that lack established solutions. Questions should probe for evidence of designing novel frameworks, tolerating sustained ambiguity, and making consequential decisions when the data will never be complete enough to eliminate uncertainty. |
| **JL4** | Redefine the problem space; navigate paradoxes; create clarity for the organization | The executive operates in an environment of structural ambiguity where the problem itself may be poorly defined. Behavioral evidence should demonstrate the ability to reframe organizational challenges, hold paradoxical tensions without premature resolution, and create strategic clarity that enables subordinate leaders to execute with confidence. |

### 5. Trade-off Sophistication

Trade-off sophistication describes the complexity of the resource allocation, prioritization, and optimization decisions the individual must make.

| Level | Trade-off Profile | Operational Implication |
|-------|-------------------|------------------------|
| **UR** | Prioritize own tasks; manage time constraints | Candidates manage simple priority conflicts within their own workload. Questions should explore how the candidate allocates limited time across competing academic or project demands. |
| **JL1** | Optimize within own workstream; escalate when tradeoffs exceed scope | The individual contributor optimizes within their defined area and recognizes when a decision exceeds their authority. Behavioral evidence should demonstrate the ability to prioritize effectively and escalate appropriately. |
| **JL2** | Make team-level resource allocation decisions; balance competing team priorities | The manager allocates finite resources (people, time, budget) across competing team priorities. Questions should probe for evidence of making explicit tradeoff decisions, communicating the rationale, and managing the consequences of deprioritization. |
| **JL3** | Make cross-functional tradeoffs; optimize at the functional level even when suboptimal for own team | The senior manager must occasionally sacrifice their own team's priorities to optimize outcomes at the functional or organizational level. Behavioral evidence should demonstrate the ability to subordinate local interests to system-level objectives and maintain team commitment despite locally unfavorable decisions. |
| **JL4** | Make enterprise-level portfolio tradeoffs; sacrifice local optima for system-wide optimization | The executive allocates across entire portfolios, business units, or geographies. Questions should probe for evidence of portfolio-level thinking, the willingness to cannibalize existing revenue streams or reorganize talent pools, and the ability to manage the cascading organizational impact of these decisions. |

## How to Adapt Interview Questions by Level

### Question Stem Adaptation

The same competency must produce fundamentally different questions at each level. Below is a worked example using the competency "Drives Results," demonstrating how the question stem shifts qualitatively across all five levels.

**Competency: Drives Results**

**UR:**
"Tell me about a group project or assignment where you were responsible for delivering a specific outcome. Walk me through how you organized your work and what the result was."

**JL1:**
"Walk me through a specific project in the last year where you were accountable for delivering a measurable result. What was the target, and how did you track progress?"

**JL2:**
"Tell me about a time in the last 18 months when your team was at risk of missing a critical deliverable. What did you do to course-correct, and what was the outcome?"

**JL3:**
"Describe a situation where you had to make tradeoffs across multiple workstreams to deliver on a high-priority business objective. How did you decide what to deprioritize?"

**JL4:**
"Walk me through a time when you had to reallocate significant resources across your portfolio to meet a shifting strategic priority. How did you manage the downstream impact on teams and stakeholders?"

Note the qualitative differences across these stems:

| Shift Dimension | UR/JL1 | JL2 | JL3 | JL4 |
|----------------|--------|-----|-----|-----|
| Agency | Personal contribution | Team course-correction | Cross-workstream tradeoffs | Portfolio-level reallocation |
| Complexity trigger | Assignment exists; execute it | Risk of failure; intervene | Competing priorities; deprioritize | Shifting strategy; restructure |
| Outcome frame | "What was the result?" | "What was the outcome?" | "How did you decide?" | "How did you manage downstream impact?" |

The progression shifts from execution (did you do the thing?) to judgment (how did you decide what to do?) to systemic management (how did you manage the second- and third-order consequences of your decision?).

### Probe Adaptation

Follow-up probes must shift in parallel with question stems. The purpose of probes is to elicit deeper behavioral evidence, and the nature of the evidence required changes by level.

**Lower Levels (UR, JL1):**
Probes focus on personal contribution, specific actions, and learning.

- "What was YOUR specific role in that project?"
- "What steps did you take when you realized you were falling behind?"
- "What did you learn from that experience that you have applied since?"
- "Who did you go to for help, and what did you ask for?"

**Mid Levels (JL2):**
Probes focus on people leadership, team dynamics, and tactical decision-making.

- "How did you diagnose what was going wrong on your team?"
- "What did you do to get your team back on track?"
- "How did you communicate the change in direction to your team?"
- "What was the impact on your team's morale, and how did you address it?"

**Upper Levels (JL3, JL4):**
Probes focus on strategic reasoning, stakeholder management, and systemic impact.

- "How did you decide between the competing priorities?"
- "What was the enterprise-level impact of that decision?"
- "How did you manage stakeholders who disagreed with your tradeoff?"
- "What did you sacrifice, and how did you mitigate the downstream consequences?"
- "How did you create alignment among leaders who had fundamentally different views on the right path forward?"
- "What signals told you it was time to shift strategy, and how did you validate those signals before acting?"

### Rubric Anchor Adaptation

A critical implementation detail: the same numeric score (e.g., "3 - Proficient") must represent different behavioral evidence at each level. Independent calibration ensures that a "3" at JL2 and a "3" at JL4 reflect level-appropriate proficiency rather than identical behavior.

**Competency: Drives Results | Score: 3 - Proficient**

| Level | Behavioral Anchor for Score of 3 |
|-------|----------------------------------|
| **UR** | The candidate describes a structured approach to organizing their work, demonstrates personal accountability for their contribution, and can articulate a clear connection between their actions and the outcome. Evidence of proactive effort even when the result was imperfect is weighted positively. |
| **JL1** | The candidate demonstrates ownership of a defined deliverable, articulates how they tracked progress against a measurable target, and provides evidence of adapting their approach when initial methods were insufficient. The outcome is clearly attributable to their individual effort. |
| **JL2** | The candidate identifies a meaningful performance risk at the team level, takes decisive corrective action through their team (not by doing all the work personally), and demonstrates the ability to communicate direction changes while maintaining team engagement. The outcome reflects improved team-level performance. |
| **JL3** | The candidate articulates a complex tradeoff across multiple workstreams, provides a defensible rationale for deprioritization decisions, demonstrates awareness of downstream functional impacts, and presents evidence that the business objective was achieved despite competing demands. |
| **JL4** | The candidate demonstrates portfolio-level resource reallocation in response to a strategic shift, articulates a sophisticated understanding of cascading organizational impacts, provides evidence of proactive stakeholder management across senior leadership, and shows measurable enterprise-level outcomes resulting from the reallocation. |

## University Recruiting Special Considerations

University recruiting candidates occupy a fundamentally different position in the assessment framework and must not be treated as "junior versions" of experienced hires. The following principles govern the adaptation of structured interview content for the UR population.

**Draw from the full range of candidate experience.** UR candidates have limited or no professional experience. Interviewers must accept behavioral evidence drawn from academic projects, internships, extracurricular activities, volunteer work, personal projects, athletic competition, and part-time employment. Question stems should be written to explicitly invite these sources (e.g., "during your studies," "in a team project or organization you were part of") rather than assuming professional context.

**Assess potential indicators, not demonstrated organizational impact.** The behavioral evidence available from UR candidates cannot demonstrate the scope, complexity, or stakeholder management required at higher levels. Instead, the interview should target indicators of future performance potential: learning agility (how quickly and effectively the candidate acquires new skills and adapts to unfamiliar situations), self-awareness (the ability to accurately assess their own strengths and development areas), initiative (evidence of taking action without being directed), and collaboration in unstructured settings (the ability to work effectively with others in the absence of formal authority or clear role definitions).

**Avoid penalizing for lack of formal leadership experience.** Many UR candidates have not held formal leadership positions. The absence of a title does not indicate the absence of leadership behavior. Questions should be written to surface informal influence, coordination, and initiative rather than requiring evidence of managing direct reports or holding positional authority.

**Anchor time references appropriately.** Use "in the last year," "during your studies," or "in a recent project" rather than "in your last role" or "in your current position." These anchors respect the candidate's actual experience base and reduce the likelihood that the candidate fabricates or inflates professional experience to match perceived expectations.

**Weight process over outcome.** UR candidates frequently operate in contexts where they execute well but have limited control over final results (a group project where a teammate did not deliver, a startup internship where the company pivoted). The rubric should weight the candidate's demonstrated process (planning, communication, adaptation, reflection) more heavily than the objective outcome, while still probing for evidence that the candidate understood and pursued a clear result.

## Cross-Level Consistency Rules

Maintaining structural consistency across levels is essential for the psychometric integrity of the interview system. The following rules must be enforced during content generation.

**Rule 1: Universal competency coverage.** The same competency must appear at every level in the interview guide. No competency should "only matter at JL4" or "not apply to UR." If a competency is strategically important enough to assess, it manifests at every level of the organization, albeit in qualitatively different ways. The structured interview generator must produce question content for all assessed competencies at all target levels.

**Rule 2: Stable competency definitions.** The behavioral definition of the competency remains constant across levels. "Drives Results" means the same thing conceptually at UR and JL4. What shifts is the level of expectation: the time horizon, scope, stakeholder complexity, ambiguity, and trade-off sophistication required to demonstrate that competency at the target level. The definition anchors consistency; the level expectations anchor differentiation.

**Rule 3: Independent rubric calibration.** Scoring rubrics at each level must be independently calibrated. A score of "3 - Proficient" at JL2 represents a fundamentally different set of behaviors than a score of "3 - Proficient" at JL4. Rubric anchors must be written independently for each level, not derived by adding qualifiers to a single anchor. Assessors must be trained to evaluate candidates against the level-appropriate rubric, not against a universal standard.

**Rule 4: Equal question depth across levels.** Coverage analysis should verify that every level receives equivalent question depth for each competency. A common failure mode is to generate rich, multi-probe questions for JL3 and JL4 while producing thin, single-probe questions for UR and JL1. Every level deserves the same structural rigor in its interview content, even though the content itself differs qualitatively.

## Common Level Differentiation Failures

The following failure modes appear frequently in competency frameworks and structured interview guides. The interview generator must actively guard against each.

**Failure 1: Adverb escalation.** This is the most pervasive and most destructive differentiation failure. It manifests as behavioral indicators that differ only in the intensity of their modifiers: "manages projects" becomes "effectively manages projects" becomes "expertly manages complex projects." This produces the illusion of differentiation while providing zero behavioral specificity. The assessor cannot reliably distinguish between levels because the indicators describe the same behavior at different perceived quality levels rather than qualitatively different behaviors. The generator must produce indicators that describe different actions, decisions, and contexts at each level, not the same actions with escalating adverbs.

**Failure 2: Scope inflation without behavioral specificity.** This failure occurs when the only difference between levels is the organizational unit referenced: "influences the team" becomes "influences the organization" becomes "influences the enterprise." While scope is a legitimate differentiator (see Dimension 2), scope alone is insufficient. The generator must specify what the individual does differently at each level to achieve that broader influence. Influencing a team of eight direct reports through daily standups is a fundamentally different behavior than influencing an enterprise of 130,000 employees through strategic communications and cascading leadership alignment. The question must elicit evidence of the specific behavior, not merely the scope.

**Failure 3: UR underspecification.** This failure treats entry-level candidates as miniature versions of experienced professionals, asking them watered-down versions of JL1 questions rather than designing questions that target the potential indicators appropriate for candidates with limited professional experience. The result is that UR interviews either penalize candidates for lacking experience they could not possibly have or fail to surface the learning agility, self-awareness, and initiative that predict future performance. The generator must produce UR content that is specifically designed for the UR population, not derived by simplifying JL1 content.

**Failure 4: JL3-JL4 collapse.** This is the most consequential differentiation failure at the senior leadership level. Many frameworks fail to distinguish between "leading a function" (JL3) and "shaping enterprise strategy" (JL4). These roles require fundamentally different behavioral evidence. A JL3 leader executes within a strategic framework established by others; a JL4 leader creates and reshapes that framework. A JL3 leader optimizes cross-functional coordination; a JL4 leader makes portfolio-level tradeoffs that may require dismantling existing functional structures. The generator must produce JL3 and JL4 content that reflects this qualitative shift from functional optimization to enterprise architecture.

**Failure 5: Ignoring stakeholder complexity.** This failure uses identical stakeholder probes across levels, asking a JL2 candidate and a JL4 candidate the same question about "managing stakeholders" without differentiating between the stakeholder environments they navigate. A JL2 candidate manages direct reports and peer managers; a JL4 candidate manages board members, regulators, and major external partners whose interests may be fundamentally opposed. The generator must calibrate stakeholder probes to reflect the specific constituencies relevant at each level, using the stakeholder complexity dimension to ensure that probes elicit level-appropriate behavioral evidence.
