# Language Profiles

Per-language guidance for the eleven standard target languages. Each profile covers: register (how formal the document voice should be), mechanics (dates, numbers, punctuation), expansion factor (how much longer/shorter text runs vs. English — drives the Phase 4 layout audit), and known traps for HR/talent content.

Expansion factors are planning guides, not laws — always verify layout empirically after replacement.

---

## Chinese, Simplified (zh-CN)

- **Register:** Professional written Chinese; avoid overly literary constructions. Second person 您 (formal "you") for instructions addressed to the reader.
- **Mechanics:** Dates `2026年7月20日`. Full-width punctuation（，。：；？）with no space before/after. Numbers use Arabic numerals with Western digit grouping in business documents. Enumeration comma 、 for in-sentence lists.
- **Expansion:** Text typically runs **20–50% shorter** than English. Watch for layouts that look sparse or vertically unbalanced; do not pad — shorter is correct.
- **Traps:** English competency jargon often has multiple competing renderings (e.g., "competency" as 能力 vs. 胜任力 — prefer 胜任力 in formal competency-model contexts, 能力 in general ability contexts; lock one in the glossary). Do not use Traditional characters (zh-TW) or Taiwan/HK terminology. STAR-type acronyms: keep the English acronym, translate the expansion once at first use.
- **Fonts:** Confirm the document's fonts include CJK coverage (e.g., Microsoft YaHei, SimSun, Noto Sans SC). If the branded font has no CJK glyphs, set CJK runs to the standard CJK companion font while leaving Latin runs in the branded font — this is the one sanctioned font deviation, and it must be applied uniformly.

## Dutch (nl-NL)

- **Register:** Formal `u` for reader-addressed instructions in HR documents (not `je`), unless the user says the company voice is informal — many NL corporate cultures do use `je`; ask once if candidate-facing.
- **Mechanics:** Dates `20 juli 2026`. Decimal comma, thousands separator `.` (e.g., `1.234,56`). Low-high quotes „..." or straight quotes acceptable; be consistent.
- **Expansion:** ~10–20% longer than English. Compound nouns can create very long unbreakable words — watch narrow table columns.
- **Traps:** English HR loanwords are common in Dutch business ("assessment", "feedback", "manager" are used as-is) — do not force-translate terms Dutch professionals use in English; the glossary decides. Capitalize only the first word of headings (no Title Case).

## French (fr-FR)

- **Register:** Formal `vous` throughout. Impersonal constructions preferred for instructions ("Veuillez indiquer…").
- **Mechanics:** Dates `20 juillet 2026` (no capital on month). Decimal comma; thin/non-breaking space as thousands separator (`1 234,56`). Non-breaking space **before** `: ; ? !` and inside guillemets « … ». Accents are mandatory including on capitals (É, À).
- **Expansion:** ~15–25% longer than English. The single most common overflow driver in this language set alongside German.
- **Traps:** Anglicisms are tolerated less than in fr-CA business writing in some domains but "feedback"/"coaching" are common — lock choices in the glossary. "Compétence" covers both "skill" and "competency"; if the source distinguishes them, establish a distinct rendering (e.g., "compétence" vs. "savoir-faire"/"aptitude") and lock it.

## French, Canadian (fr-CA)

- **Register:** Formal `vous`. Same structural rules as fr-FR.
- **Mechanics:** Dates `20 juillet 2026`. Decimal comma, space thousands separator. Unlike fr-FR, **no space before** `; ? !` (space before `:` is retained). Guillemets « » with non-breaking spaces.
- **Traps:** fr-CA business language resists anglicisms more strongly than fr-FR ("courriel" not "e-mail", "clavardage", "gestionnaire" for manager). OQLF-aligned terminology is expected in workplace documents. **Never ship the fr-FR file as fr-CA** — vocabulary, punctuation spacing, and anglicism policy all differ. Employment-document language in Canada may carry legal weight (Quebec Bill 96 context: French versions of employment documents are often the legally required version) — flag this to the user for candidate-facing material.

## German (de-DE)

- **Register:** Formal `Sie` throughout. Nominal style is natural in German business writing but do not let it obscure actionability of instructions.
- **Mechanics:** Dates `20. Juli 2026`. Decimal comma, `.` thousands separator (`1.234,56`). Quotes „…". All nouns capitalized.
- **Expansion:** **~20–35% longer** than English, with long compound nouns (e.g., "Kompetenzentwicklungsplan") that will not wrap inside narrow table cells or chart labels. The highest-risk language for layout breakage — audit every table and text box.
- **Traps:** Gender-inclusive language: German corporate standards vary (generic masculine vs. paired forms "Mitarbeiterinnen und Mitarbeiter" vs. neutral forms "Mitarbeitende"). Default to neutral forms where natural (Mitarbeitende, Führungskräfte, Teilnehmende); ask the user if the document is policy-level. English HR terms ("Feedback", "Assessment Center", "Onboarding") are standard German business vocabulary — keep them where a German HR professional would.

## Indonesian, Bahasa Indonesia (id-ID)

- **Register:** Formal written Indonesian; address the reader as `Anda` (capitalized). Avoid Jakarta colloquialisms.
- **Mechanics:** Dates `20 Juli 2026`. Decimal comma, `.` thousands separator. No grammatical plural marking — do not reduplicate nouns to force plurals where context suffices.
- **Expansion:** Roughly parity to ~15% longer than English.
- **Traps:** Distinguish Indonesian from Malay (ms-MY) — related but not interchangeable; several everyday words differ or shift meaning. English HR/tech loanwords are widely used; the glossary decides between loanword and native term (e.g., "kompetensi" is standard for competency). Formal affixed verb forms (me-/di-) belong in professional documents.

## Polish (pl-PL)

- **Register:** Formal address via `Pan/Pani/Państwo` constructions or impersonal forms ("Proszę wskazać…") — impersonal is often smoothest for instructions. Never informal `ty` in HR documents.
- **Mechanics:** Dates `20 lipca 2026` (month genitive, lowercase). Decimal comma, space thousands separator. Quotes „…".
- **Expansion:** ~15–25% longer than English.
- **Traps:** Rich inflection means glossary terms must be locked as **lemmas** and inflected correctly in context — a consistency scan must match stems, not exact strings. Gendered job titles and participles: prefer forms that don't force a gender on the reader where possible, or use paired/neutral phrasing for candidate-facing text.

## Portuguese, Brazilian (pt-BR)

- **Register:** `você` with formal-professional tone is standard in Brazilian corporate writing (unlike pt-PT, which leans on third-person/`o senhor` structures). Direct imperatives are acceptable in instructions.
- **Mechanics:** Dates `20 de julho de 2026`. Decimal comma, `.` thousands separator. Quotes "…" (English-style curly quotes are standard in Brazil).
- **Expansion:** ~15–25% longer than English.
- **Traps:** Confirm the variant at intake — pt-BR and pt-PT differ in vocabulary, pronoun placement, and spelling even post-Acordo Ortográfico; never present one as covering both. "Cargo" (position) vs. "função" (function/role) vs. "competência" mappings should be locked in the glossary.

## Russian (ru-RU)

- **Register:** Formal `Вы` (commonly capitalized in direct address in business correspondence; consistent lowercase `вы` is also acceptable in modern corporate style — pick one). Impersonal/infinitive constructions are natural for instructions.
- **Mechanics:** Dates `20 июля 2026 г.`. Decimal comma, space thousands separator. Quotes «…». Month names lowercase.
- **Expansion:** ~15–30% longer than English; Cyrillic also renders wider per character in many fonts — treat like German for layout risk.
- **Traps:** Confirm the branded fonts carry Cyrillic glyphs (most standard corporate fonts do; decorative ones may not — apply the same companion-font rule as zh-CN if not). Aspect (perfective/imperfective) changes instruction meaning — "rate the candidate" as a one-time completed action wants perfective. HR anglicisms ("компетенция", "ассессмент", "фидбек") vary in acceptance; "компетенция" is standard, but prefer native renderings over raw transliterations where a standard term exists.

## Spanish, European (es-ES)

- **Register:** `usted` for candidate-facing formality, though modern Spanish corporate voice often uses `tú` internally — ask once; default `usted` for assessment/interview materials.
- **Mechanics:** Dates `20 de julio de 2026`. Decimal comma, `.` thousands separator. Opening ¿ ¡ are mandatory on questions/exclamations. Quotes «…» traditional; "…" acceptable.
- **Expansion:** ~15–25% longer than English.
- **Traps:** "Vosotros" forms are es-ES-only — their presence in an es-419 file (or absence where es-ES informal plural is needed) is the classic variant-mixing defect. Distinct vocabulary from es-419 ("ordenador" vs. "computadora", "currículum"/"CV" conventions).

## Spanish, Latin American (es-419)

- **Register:** `usted` in formal HR documents; `ustedes` is the only second-person plural (never vosotros).
- **Mechanics:** Dates `20 de julio de 2026`. **Number formats vary by country** — Mexico uses decimal point (`1,234.56`), the Southern Cone uses decimal comma. For a pan-regional document, prefer the decimal point with comma grouping (Mexico/Central America convention) or spell numbers out where ambiguity matters; state the choice in the QA log.
- **Expansion:** ~15–25% longer than English.
- **Traps:** es-419 is a neutral pan-regional register, not a country dialect — avoid country-specific colloquialisms (Mexicanisms, Argentinisms) and choose the most widely understood term ("computadora", "manejar" vs "conducir" — prefer neutral). Never ship the es-ES file as es-419 or vice versa: pronoun systems, vocabulary, and register conventions differ.

## Cross-Language Rules

1. **Acronyms with meaning** (STAR, BEI, BARS, KPI): keep the English acronym; translate the spelled-out form at first occurrence — `BEI (entrevista de eventos conductuales)` — then use the acronym alone, matching the English document's pattern.
2. **Rating-scale anchors** must be translated as a *scale*: renderings must preserve the ordinal distance between anchors ("Exceeds" must clearly outrank "Meets" in the target language), and the same anchor set must render identically across every file in the set.
3. **Proper nouns and product names** stay English unless the glossary says otherwise.
4. **Names of laws/frameworks** (e.g., SIOP Principles, Uniform Guidelines): keep the official English name; add a brief translated gloss in parentheses at first use if the audience won't recognize it.
5. **Untranslatable idioms:** replace with the target-language expression carrying the same force — never translate word-for-word, never leave English idiom fragments.
6. **RTL languages** (Arabic, Hebrew) are outside the standard eleven; if requested, layout mirroring is required and the format-fidelity workload roughly doubles — set expectations at intake.
