## Section 1: Project Overview

### Purpose Statement
```
The Technical Competency Builder (TCB) initiative is developing technical competencies across Cargill's 15 job families to support talent decisions spanning selection, development, performance management, and succession planning. These competencies form the third layer of Cargill's three-tier competency architecture:

  Layer 1 — Values & Behaviors: Enterprise-wide expectations aligned to Cargill's corporate values and 2030 strategic goals. Apply to all employees.

  Layer 2 — Common Competencies: Cross-functional capabilities expected across multiple job families (e.g., business acumen, stakeholder management).

  Layer 3 — Technical Competencies: Specialized knowledge, skills, and behaviors unique to a job family or specialization. This is the layer under review today.

Your role as a Subject Matter Expert (SME) is to evaluate whether the draft technical competencies accurately represent the critical knowledge, skills, and behaviors required for effective performance in the {{SPECIALIZATION_NAME}} specialization within Cargill Legal & Compliance.
```

### What We Are Asking You to Do
```
During this 60-minute structured session, you will:

  1. Review each draft technical competency and its behavioral indicators across four proficiency levels (L1 through L4).

  2. Rate each competency on three dimensions:
     — Relevance: How important is this competency to effective job performance in {{SPECIALIZATION_NAME}}?
     — Clarity: Are the behavioral indicators clearly written and unambiguous?
     — Level Differentiation: Do the L1-L4 indicators represent meaningfully different levels of proficiency?

  3. Identify gaps: Are there critical technical capabilities missing from the draft set?

  4. Validate the mapping between competencies and essential job functions for the Manager II and Advisor bands.

Your expert judgment directly shapes the final competency framework. This is not a test — there are no right or wrong answers. We need your candid professional assessment based on your experience performing and supervising this work.
```

## Section 2: Focus Group Protocol

### Session Structure
```
The 60-minute session follows this structure:

  Minutes 0-5: Welcome, introductions, and overview of the session purpose and ground rules.

  Minutes 5-15: Walkthrough of the competency architecture (three-layer model) and explanation of the rating scales. Questions and clarifications.

  Minutes 15-50: Competency-by-competency review. For each competency, the facilitator will:
    — Read the competency name and definition aloud.
    — Allow 2-3 minutes for silent individual review of the L1-L4 behavioral indicators.
    — Facilitate group discussion on relevance, clarity, and level differentiation.
    — Capture specific wording suggestions, additions, or deletions.
    — Record individual ratings on the feedback form.

  Minutes 50-55: Gap analysis — open discussion on whether any critical technical capabilities are missing from the draft set.

  Minutes 55-60: Wrap-up, next steps, and timeline for incorporating feedback.
```

### Ground Rules
```
  — Every perspective is valuable. Disagreement among SMEs is expected and informative.
  — Focus on the work, not the person. We are evaluating whether behaviors are accurately described, not whether individuals possess them.
  — Specificity helps. "This indicator is vague" is less actionable than "This indicator should specify whether we mean internal investigations or regulatory investigations."
  — Silence is data. If a competency or indicator generates no discussion, that signals clarity and consensus.
  — We will capture all feedback. If time runs short on a competency, we will collect written feedback via the companion form.
```

## Section 3: Rating Scale Definitions

### Relevance Scale (5-point)
```
How important is this competency to effective job performance in {{SPECIALIZATION_NAME}}?

  5 — Essential: This competency is critical. Deficiency would result in unacceptable job performance.
  4 — Important: This competency significantly contributes to effective performance. Most incumbents need it.
  3 — Useful: This competency is helpful but not critical. Some incumbents may perform adequately without strong proficiency.
  2 — Minor relevance: This competency has limited applicability to {{SPECIALIZATION_NAME}} work.
  1 — Not relevant: This competency does not apply to {{SPECIALIZATION_NAME}}.
```

### Clarity Scale (3-point)
```
Are the behavioral indicators clearly written and unambiguous?

  3 — Clear: The indicators are specific, observable, and would be interpreted consistently by different raters.
  2 — Partially clear: The general intent is understandable, but some indicators are vague or could be interpreted differently by different raters. Suggest specific rewording.
  1 — Unclear: The indicators are too abstract, jargon-heavy, or ambiguous to be useful. Significant rewriting needed.
```

### Level Differentiation Scale (3-point)
```
Do the L1-L4 indicators represent meaningfully different levels of proficiency?

  3 — Well differentiated: Each level describes qualitatively different behavior. A trained observer could reliably distinguish an L2 performer from an L3 performer using these indicators.
  2 — Partially differentiated: Some levels are distinct, but others overlap or differ only in degree (e.g., "manages" vs. "effectively manages"). Suggest where the gaps are.
  1 — Not differentiated: The levels read as minor variations of the same behavior. The progression from L1 to L4 is not meaningful. Fundamental restructuring needed.
```

## Section 4: Competency Architecture Reference

### Three-Layer Model Overview
```
Cargill's competency architecture operates on three integrated layers:

  Layer 1 — Values & Behaviors
  Enterprise-wide. Derived from Cargill's corporate values and aligned to the 2030 strategic goals. These define "how we work" at Cargill regardless of function or level. Applied universally in performance management and cultural alignment.

  Layer 2 — Common Competencies
  Cross-functional. Capabilities like business acumen, stakeholder management, and strategic thinking that span multiple job families. Differentiated by career level (Individual Contributor through Senior Manager 1+).

  Layer 3 — Technical Competencies (Under Review Today)
  Specialization-specific. The knowledge, skills, and behaviors that distinguish effective performance within a particular professional discipline. These competencies reflect what makes a strong {{SPECIALIZATION_NAME}} professional specifically — beyond the general leadership and business capabilities captured in Layers 1 and 2.

Today's review focuses exclusively on Layer 3 content.
```

### Proficiency Level Definitions
```
Technical competencies are scaled across four proficiency levels that reflect qualitative shifts in scope, complexity, and autonomy:

  L1 — Foundational
  Applies core technical knowledge under guidance. Handles routine matters within established frameworks. Seeks direction on novel or complex situations. Typical of early-career professionals or those new to the specialization.

  L2 — Practitioner
  Independently executes the full range of standard technical work. Identifies and resolves moderately complex issues. Begins to advise others on technical matters. Exercises judgment within established precedent.

  L3 — Advanced Practitioner
  Handles complex, ambiguous, or high-stakes technical matters with minimal oversight. Shapes technical strategy within a domain. Mentors and develops less experienced professionals. Recognized as a go-to expert within the function.

  L4 — Expert / Thought Leader
  Defines and drives technical strategy across the enterprise or externally. Navigates unprecedented, multi-jurisdictional, or enterprise-risk-level matters. Shapes organizational capability and external reputation. Influences industry standards, regulatory frameworks, or professional practice.
```

### Band Mapping Reference
```
Cargill career bands relevant to this review:

  Job Level 1 (JL1) — Entry-level professional. Typically maps to L1 proficiency.
  Job Level 2 (JL2) — Experienced professional. Typically maps to L1-L2 proficiency.
  Job Level 3 (JL3) — Senior professional / Manager I. Typically maps to L2-L3 proficiency.
  Job Level 4 (JL4) — Director / Manager II. Typically maps to L3-L4 proficiency.
  University Recruiting (UR) — Pre-professional. Typically maps to foundational L1 exposure.

  The two primary bands under review today are:
  — Manager II (JL4 equivalent): Expected to operate at L3-L4 across most technical competencies.
  — Advisor (senior individual contributor): Expected to operate at L3-L4 with deep technical depth.

  Essential functions for each band are provided in Section 6 for your reference during the competency review.
```

## Section 7: Validation Methodology Note

### For SME Reference
```
The validation approach used in this project follows established content validity methodology as specified by the Society for Industrial and Organizational Psychology (SIOP) Principles for the Validation and Use of Personnel Selection Procedures and the American Psychological Association (APA) Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing.

Content validity evidence is established when qualified Subject Matter Experts confirm that the competency content is representative of the critical requirements of the job. Your individual ratings will be aggregated to compute a Content Validity Ratio (CVR) for each competency, following Lawshe's (1975) methodology. Competencies meeting the CVR threshold for your panel size will be retained; those falling below will be revised or removed.

This process ensures that the final competency framework is legally defensible, psychometrically grounded, and practically meaningful for talent decisions.
```

## Appendix: Glossary of Terms

```
Behavioral Indicator — A specific, observable action or behavior that demonstrates proficiency in a competency at a given level.

Competency — A cluster of related knowledge, skills, and behaviors that differentiate levels of job performance.

Content Validity — Evidence that an assessment or framework adequately represents the critical requirements of the job, as judged by subject matter experts.

Content Validity Ratio (CVR) — A statistical index (Lawshe, 1975) quantifying the degree of SME agreement that a competency is essential to job performance. Ranges from -1 to +1, with higher values indicating stronger agreement.

Essential Function — A fundamental duty or responsibility of a job, as defined in the job description. Distinguished from marginal functions by frequency, importance, and consequences of non-performance.

Job Level (JL) — Cargill's career band designation (JL1 through JL4) reflecting scope, complexity, and organizational impact.

Observable-Measurable-Discriminant (OMD) — Quality criteria for behavioral indicators: observable (can be seen or documented), measurable (can be rated consistently), discriminant (differs meaningfully across proficiency levels).

Proficiency Level (L1-L4) — A qualitative tier of capability within a technical competency, ranging from Foundational (L1) to Expert/Thought Leader (L4).

Subject Matter Expert (SME) — An individual with deep professional experience and expertise in the specialization under review, qualified to evaluate the relevance and accuracy of competency content.

Technical Competency — A Layer 3 competency capturing specialized knowledge, skills, and behaviors unique to a job family or specialization, distinct from enterprise values (Layer 1) and common competencies (Layer 2).
```
